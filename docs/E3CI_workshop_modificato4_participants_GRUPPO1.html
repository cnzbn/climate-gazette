How to use this notebook

  • Work directly in this notebook and save it with File → Save (Ctrl + S): don’t make a copy, otherwise your work won’t reach the Climate Gazette. If Colab warns that the notebook was “not authored by Google”, click Run anyway.
  • First run the five Setup cells, from top to bottom: click ▶ on the left of a cell, or press Shift + Enter.
  • To change a chart: use the form at the top of its cell (✏️ Settings of this chart). Choose from the menus and sliders, write your title in TITLE (or leave it empty for the automatic title), then run the cell again (▶). Only the settings in the form can be changed: the code below the line “How the chart is made” stays as it is.
  • To add your text: double-click a text cell to edit it, or add a new one with + Text.
  • Something went wrong? Read the last line of the red message. If Colab restarted, click the cell you were on and use Runtime → Run before.
  • Every chart is also saved in the folder my_figures (📁 icon on the left).

How to read the E3CI in one minute

  • For every month, place and component, the E3CI compares that month with the same month in 1981–2010. It is a standardised anomaly: 0 = a normal month, 1 = one standard deviation above normal.
  • Following the E3CI Data Station, values above 1 are extreme, in three classes: 1–2, 2–3, above 3.
  • Some values reach the hundreds, even about 2,000. Where an event almost never happened in 1981–2010 (heavy rain in a desert summer, fire weather in Ireland), even a moderate event looks gigantic on this scale. That’s one reason why the Data Station shows classes rather than raw numbers.
  • Fire is not computed where and when fire danger practically never occurs (e.g. winter in northern Europe): those months are empty, and fire statistics use only the months in which it is computed.

Data: E3CI – European Extreme Events Climate Index, climateindex.eu, computed from the Copernicus Climate Change Service ERA5 reanalysis. Explore it at the Data Station.

1.2 When in the year?

A rose for each hazard and period, inspired by Florence Nightingale’s diagrams (1858). How to read it: * every petal is a calendar month: January at the top, then clockwise (April on the right, July at the bottom, October on the left); * the area of a petal, not its length, is proportional to the share of country-months that were extreme in that month, as in Nightingale’s roses; * inside each petal the classes go from the centre outwards: 1–2 (the lightest shade), 2–3, above 3 (the darkest); * top row: the first period, bottom row: the second one (chosen in PERIODS).

Remember: every month is compared with the same month of 1981–2010, so an extreme in winter is unusual for winter.

💬 Compare the two rows: a rose that grows in every direction means more extremes all year round; a petal that grows alone points to one season.

3.3 Records: are they falling faster than chance would predict?

The same months, seen through their records. A record is a month with a higher E3CI than every month before it in the same country, since January 1981. In a climate that does not change, records become rarer and rarer: after 10 years the chance that a month beats all the previous ones is about 1 in 120; after 40 years, about 1 in 480. That’s why records are counted only from START_YEAR (1991 by default): in the first years almost every month is a record, because there is hardly any history to beat.

📐 How many records should we expect? In a stable climate each of the first n months has the same chance of being the highest, so month n sets a record with probability 1/n. Adding up 1/n over the months since START_YEAR gives the number of records we would expect if nothing were changing. It is a reference, not an exact forecast: anomalies persist from one month to the next, so records can come in clusters.

💬 Compare the records observed with those expected in a stable climate (in the title): many more records than chance, especially in the last years, are a sign of a changing climate.
